# Elias Mercer

**Full Name:** Elias Mercer  
**Known As:** Inspector Mercer  
**Species:** [[Humans|Human]]  
**Gender:** Male  
**Age:** 42  
**Faction:** [[Protection and Crime Enforcement]]  
**Rank:** Inspector  
**Assignment:** Criminal Investigations Division  
**Position:** Detective / Senior Investigator  
**Specialisation:** Homicide, Organised Crime, Complex Criminal Investigations  
**Status:** Active  

**Elias Mercer** is an Inspector and experienced detective within the [[Protection and Crime Enforcement|Protection and Crime Enforcement]], specialising in homicide, organised crime, and complex investigations spanning multiple jurisdictions.

Mercer has built his career around a simple principle:

**Everybody leaves something behind.**

A fingerprint. A transaction. A witness. A damaged machine. A deleted message. A lie told slightly differently the second time.

Mercer's job is finding it.

## Appearance

Mercer is a human male in his early forties with a lean, slightly worn appearance.

He has dark hair beginning to grey around the temples, brown eyes, and usually maintains short facial stubble.

Unlike Tactical Response personnel, Mercer rarely wears heavy armour during ordinary investigations.

He generally dresses in practical civilian clothing beneath a lightweight PCE protective vest or coat, carrying his identification and equipment visibly when required.

Mercer frequently looks tired.

This is because he frequently is.

## Personality

Mercer is patient, analytical, dry-humoured, and extremely persistent.

He rarely intimidates suspects deliberately.

He prefers allowing them to talk.

People uncomfortable with silence frequently attempt to fill it.

Mercer is perfectly happy to let them.

He pays close attention to apparently insignificant inconsistencies in conversations and has an excellent memory for statements made during investigations.

He does not possess a supernatural ability to detect lies.

People can fool him.

Mercer simply understands that maintaining a lie becomes increasingly difficult when somebody keeps asking the right questions.

His patience has irritated suspects, witnesses, criminals, colleagues, and occasionally his superiors.

Mercer considers this acceptable.

## Career

Mercer began his PCE career as a conventional Officer.

Several years of patrol work eventually led him toward criminal investigation, where his attention to detail and unusually patient interviewing style proved particularly effective.

He progressed through investigative assignments before eventually reaching the rank of Inspector.

Mercer now handles major criminal investigations and may supervise smaller investigative teams during complex cases.

His work has included homicide, kidnapping, organised crime, trafficking, corruption, serial offences, criminal conspiracies, and cases extending across multiple worlds.

Some investigations have lasted hours.

Others have lasted years.

Mercer remembers all of them.

## Criminal Investigations Division

The Criminal Investigations Division handles offences requiring investigation beyond the capabilities or responsibilities of ordinary patrol personnel.

Investigators gather evidence, interview witnesses, identify suspects, reconstruct events, coordinate forensic analysis, and prepare cases for prosecution.

Mercer primarily works major investigations.

A typical case may involve cooperation between patrol officers, forensic specialists, intelligence analysts, cybercrime personnel, Tactical Response, customs authorities, and law-enforcement agencies operating on other worlds.

Mercer's responsibility is often making those separate pieces form a coherent picture.

## Investigative Method

Mercer dislikes beginning an investigation with a theory.

He begins with evidence.

He reconstructs events chronologically whenever possible:

Who arrived?

Who left?

What happened?

What should have happened?

What changed?

Who benefited?

And, frequently most importantly:

**What does somebody desperately want PCE to believe happened?**

Mercer is particularly cautious around evidence that appears almost too useful.

An obvious suspect is still investigated.

A convenient confession is still verified.

A weapon found beside a body does not automatically belong to the killer.

Technology has made fabricating evidence considerably easier than it once was.

Mercer believes this has made old-fashioned scepticism considerably more valuable.

## Interviews

Mercer's interview style is notably calm.

He rarely shouts.

He rarely threatens.

He asks questions.

Then he asks them again differently.

Then several hours later, when the person being interviewed has forgotten exactly what they said the first time, Mercer asks again.

He allows long periods of silence.

He changes apparently irrelevant details.

He returns unexpectedly to earlier subjects.

He compares statements against physical and digital evidence.

Mercer understands that innocent people can misremember events and guilty people can tell the truth.

Contradictions therefore interest him.

They do not automatically convince him.

## Technology and Forensics

Modern investigations throughout the Omicron Galaxy can involve enormous quantities of information.

Surveillance networks, communication records, spacecraft logs, biometric systems, financial transactions, synthetic memory, weapon telemetry, environmental sensors, and countless other systems can potentially provide evidence.

Mercer uses these resources extensively.

He does not trust them blindly.

Systems can malfunction.

Records can be altered.

AIs can be deceived.

Android memories can be manipulated.

Surveillance can show exactly what happened while completely failing to explain why.

Mercer considers technology an investigative tool rather than an oracle.

## Synthetic Crime

Androids, robots, artificial intelligences, cybernetic augmentation, and consciousness-related technologies have created categories of crime that earlier law-enforcement systems were never designed to handle.

Mercer has participated in investigations involving synthetic persons both as suspects and victims.

He strongly rejects the assumption that synthetic involvement automatically makes a case a technical problem rather than a criminal one.

If an android is murdered, Mercer treats it as a homicide wherever local law recognises the individual as a legal person.

If an AI is accused of an offence, he expects evidence.

If a machine has been remotely compromised, he wants to know who compromised it.

This has occasionally placed Mercer inside legal disputes considerably larger than the case he originally intended to investigate.

## Organised Crime

Mercer's work frequently brings him into contact with organised criminal networks operating across planetary and interstellar borders.

These organisations may exploit differences between jurisdictions, hide assets on distant worlds, use shell corporations, employ mercenaries, or move contraband through legitimate trade networks.

Mercer finds these investigations particularly frustrating.

He also finds them particularly satisfying.

Arresting the person carrying contraband solves one crime.

Finding the organisation that put it in their hands can solve hundreds.

## Relationship with Officer Reyes

Mercer has encountered [[Maya Reyes|Officer Maya Reyes]] during several investigations originating from patrol calls.

He considers Reyes observant and appreciates that she records details which initially appear irrelevant.

He has also repeatedly advised her that curiosity is considerably safer when accompanied by backup.

Reyes has repeatedly agreed.

This has not entirely solved the problem.

Mercer nevertheless trusts her observations and has occasionally requested her involvement when an investigation concerns people or locations she regularly encounters while on patrol.

## Relationship with Chief Inspector Ward

Mercer works with [[Elena Ward|Chief Inspector Elena Ward]] when investigations require high-risk arrests or Tactical Response involvement.

The two generally work well together.

Mercer wants suspects alive because dead people are notoriously difficult to interview.

Ward generally wants suspects alive because her job is law enforcement rather than warfare.

Problems arise when Mercer wants another hour to gather evidence and Ward believes waiting another hour creates unacceptable danger.

Neither automatically wins that argument.

## Relationship with Commissioner Thompson

Mercer's relationship with [[Gareth Thompson|Commissioner Gareth Thompson]] is professional but occasionally strained.

Mercer respects Thompson's experience and opposition to corruption.

He is considerably less comfortable with the Commissioner's willingness to employ extreme methods during major crises.

The disagreement is principally procedural rather than personal.

Thompson is sometimes required to think about what prevents immediate catastrophe.

Mercer has to think about what can eventually be proven.

Mercer believes strongly that pressure to produce an answer must never become pressure to manufacture one.

A suspect being obviously guilty is not sufficient.

PCE must be able to demonstrate it.

## Corruption

Mercer has an intense dislike of corruption within law enforcement.

A corrupt PCE officer possesses access, authority, training, information, and institutional trust that ordinary criminals do not.

Mercer therefore considers crimes committed using PCE authority particularly damaging.

He has assisted Internal Affairs investigations when criminal cases intersected with suspected police corruption.

He does not enjoy investigating other officers.

He will do it anyway.

The badge does not make evidence disappear.

## Weapons and Equipment

Mercer carries considerably less equipment than a Tactical Response Officer.

His typical equipment includes:

- P2-series service pistol
- PCE identification
- Lightweight protective equipment
- Portable forensic scanner
- Evidence collection equipment
- Secure recording system
- Communications equipment
- Restraints
- Personal data terminal

He is qualified with larger PCE weapons but rarely carries them during routine investigative work.

Mercer is a competent shooter.

He would strongly prefer that shooting never become the most important part of an investigation.

## Anomalous Cases

Mercer dislikes anomalous investigations.

Not because he refuses to believe anomalous phenomena exist.

Quite the opposite.

They make evidence extraordinarily irritating.

An impossible event remains an event.

Something happened.

Something caused it.

Someone witnessed part of it.

Some physical consequence was produced.

Mercer therefore approaches anomalous crime using the same principle he applies elsewhere:

Document what can be demonstrated.

Separate observation from assumption.

Do not invent an explanation merely because the real one is currently unavailable.

This attitude has occasionally brought Mercer into contact with investigations eventually transferred far beyond conventional PCE authority.

He keeps copies of every report he is legally permitted to retain.

## Unsolved Cases

Mercer maintains a private record of investigations he considers unresolved.

Some are officially open.

Others have been administratively closed due to insufficient evidence, jurisdictional problems, missing suspects, political complications, or circumstances outside PCE control.

Mercer does not obsessively pursue every cold case at the expense of current responsibilities.

But he does not forget them.

Occasionally, years later, a new arrest produces a familiar name.

A recovered weapon matches an old forensic report.

A dead criminal possesses information relating to someone who disappeared decades earlier.

When that happens, Mercer opens the file again.

## Reputation

Mercer has a reputation within PCE for being exceptionally difficult to discourage once he believes a crime can be solved.

He is not the organisation's greatest fighter.

He is not its highest-ranking officer.

He has no anomalous abilities and possesses no extraordinary technology unavailable to other investigators.

He simply keeps looking.

Criminals have escaped Mercer.

Suspects have successfully lied to him.

Cases have gone cold.

Evidence has disappeared.

Jurisdictions have refused cooperation.

Sometimes there genuinely has not been enough information to determine what happened.

Mercer accepts that.

What he does not accept is confusing **difficult to prove** with **not worth proving**.

Everybody leaves something behind.

Mercer is willing to wait until he finds it.
